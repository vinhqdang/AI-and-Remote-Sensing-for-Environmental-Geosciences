# Literature review: cross-event transferability of coseismic landslide models

All citations below are BibTeX keys in `references.bib`. Every key was checked against the Crossref API (DOI resolves; title, first author and year match). Where a claim depends on a specific reference, the key is given right after the claim.

## 1. Coseismic landslide inventories as the empirical basis

Earthquake-triggered landsliding has been described quantitatively since Keefer's compilation of 40 historical earthquakes. That work set out the magnitude dependence of the area affected and the minimum shaking needed for different landslide types [keefer1984]. Keefer later reviewed how investigation methods moved from field reconnaissance to aerial and satellite mapping [keefer2002]. Fan et al. treat coseismic landslides as the first link in a cascade of hazards (landslide dams, debris flows, elevated post-seismic sediment flux) that continues for years after the mainshock [fan2019]. Coseismic landslide prediction is therefore a process problem as well as a mapping problem.

Statistical modelling depends on event inventories, and their quality varies widely. Tanyaş et al. compiled a worldwide database of earthquake-induced landslide inventories and found large differences in completeness, mapping method and polygon versus point representation [tanyas2017]. The USGS ground-failure repository made many of these inventories openly available in a common format [schmitt2017]. It is the practical source for multi-event studies such as ours. The nine events we study are documented in this literature:

- 1994 Northridge [harp1996]
- 2002 Denali, where landsliding was sparser than the magnitude would suggest and was used to infer the character of the strong shaking [jibson2004]
- 2004 Mid-Niigata [sato2005]
- 2008 Iwate–Miyagi Nairiku [yagi2009]
- 2007 Aysén Fjord [sepulveda2010]
- 2009 Cinchona, where shaking-triggered slides turned into mudflows [alvarado2009]
- 2016 Kaikōura [massey2018; massey2020update; massey2020volume]
- 2018 Papua New Guinea (Hela), described as the second-largest recorded landslide event, with continued landsliding after the mainshock [tanyas2022png]
- 2018 Palu [bradley2019palu; shao2023palu]

These events differ in ways that matter for transfer. Palu's largest failures were long-runout flowslides on very gentle slopes, made possible by irrigation for wet-rice cultivation [bradley2019palu]. They do not follow the steep-slope, high-shaking pattern that most models learn. Kaikōura's landslides were concentrated by a complex multi-fault rupture [hamling2017], and its strong-motion records show strong spatial variability [bradley2017]. Its inventory was revised from more than 10,000 to roughly 30,000 landslides as mapping improved [massey2018; massey2020update]. A model evaluated against such inventories is therefore partly evaluated against the mapping protocol. This matters when a model is transferred to an event whose inventory was produced differently [tanyas2017; steger2016].

## 2. Physics-based estimation: the Newmark sliding block

Newmark's rigid sliding-block analysis treats a potential landslide as a block that moves whenever ground acceleration exceeds a critical (yield) acceleration. Permanent displacement accumulates by double integration of the excess [newmark1965]. Jibson and colleagues made this a regional method. They combined a DEM-derived slope with geotechnical strength to estimate critical acceleration and used empirical relations to estimate displacement from shaking. They then calibrated the probability of failure against the Northridge inventory, producing digital probabilistic seismic landslide hazard maps [jibson2000]. Regression models now predict Newmark displacement from the critical-acceleration ratio and PGA, Arias intensity or magnitude [jibson2007]. Saygili & Rathje showed that vector predictors combining PGA and PGV reduce displacement uncertainty [saygili2008]. In his retrospective, Jibson places the Newmark approach between pseudo-static and full stress-deformation analysis, as a practical index of seismic slope performance rather than an exact prediction [jibson2011].

Newmark displacement is the natural physics feature for our models for three reasons. It encodes the monotonic expectation that failure probability rises with shaking and slope. It has a mechanistic interpretation. And its calibration to probability is known to be event-specific: the Northridge calibration curve [jibson2000] has no guarantee of holding for another event. Our physics baseline tests that last point directly.

## 3. Global and near-real-time empirical models

Near-real-time (NRT) systems need predictions within hours of an earthquake, before landslides can be mapped. Nowicki et al. built a globally applicable logistic-regression model that combines ShakeMap PGA with slope and other static predictors [nowicki2014]. Nowicki Jessee et al. extended it to 23 inventories with PGV, slope, lithology, land cover and a wetness index [nowickijessee2018]. It became the core of the USGS ground-failure product, which has been operational alongside ShakeMap and PAGER [allstadt2022]. The PGA and PGV fields come from ShakeMap. ShakeMap began as rapid TriNet maps [wald1999] and now conditions ground-motion models on station observations with spatial and spectral cross-correlation [worden2018]. The ground-motion inputs therefore carry event-specific uncertainty that changes with station density, a direct source of covariate shift between events.

Other multi-event or inventory-free approaches include:

- Kritikos et al. fitted fuzzy-logic membership functions on several past earthquakes and applied them to events whose landslides were not used in training, which is an explicit cross-event design [kritikos2015].
- Tanyaş et al. used slope units with global multi-event training [tanyas2019slope].
- Lee developed a statistical seismic landslide hazard analysis for Taiwan [lee2014].
- Lombardo & Tanyaş proposed "chrono-validation": a model calibrated on past events is used to predict a later event [lombardo2020chrono].
- Li et al. applied a regression model developed for the 2008 Wenchuan earthquake to the 2013 Lushan earthquake for rapid susceptibility mapping [li2013lushan]. This is a direct, single-pair example of cross-event transfer.
- Xu et al. showed that a logistic model trained on an incomplete Wenchuan inventory can still produce useful susceptibility maps [xu2013incomplete]. This supports the premise that a small number of early-mapped landslides is informative.

Kaikōura is the most relevant test case for a NZ audience. Allstadt et al. compared candidate USGS models against the Kaikōura inventory and drew lessons for NRT model design. Their lessons include sensitivity to the ground-motion input and to which predictors are used [allstadt2018]. Robinson et al. showed for Kaikōura that NRT modelling of landslide impacts can inform rapid response [robinson2018kaikoura]. For the 2015 Gorkha earthquake they examined how coseismic landslide models perform in the emergency phase, when inventories are incomplete [robinson2017]. Recent work replaces scalar ShakeMap intensity measures with simulated ground motion [dahal2023groundmotion] or full seismic waveforms processed by transformer networks [dahal2024waveform]. That work reports gains in coseismic landslide prediction, which implies that part of the transfer error lies in the shaking representation and not only in the terrain model.

## 4. Statistical and machine-learning susceptibility modelling

Reichenbach et al. reviewed several hundred statistically based susceptibility studies. They found logistic regression the most common method and identified widespread problems with validation and with reporting model uncertainty [reichenbach2018]. Budimir et al. reviewed logistic-regression probability mapping specifically, including its earthquake-triggered applications [budimir2015]. Merghadi et al. compared ML algorithms and found that tree ensembles generally reach the highest discrimination [merghadi2020]. Gradient boosting [friedman2001] in its scalable XGBoost form [chen2016] is now a default learner. Lombardo et al. argued for space-time models that treat triggers explicitly instead of static susceptibility alone [lombardo2020spacetime]. This framing fits coseismic prediction, where each earthquake is a separate "time slice."

Validation design largely determines how optimistic reported performance is. Random cross-validation on spatially autocorrelated data inflates accuracy. Spatial and block cross-validation give more honest estimates [brenning2012; roberts2017]. Roberts et al. specifically recommend blocking on the structure across which prediction is intended [roberts2017], and for coseismic transfer that structure is the event. Meyer & Pebesma showed that predictions in feature space far from the training data are unreliable and proposed the "area of applicability" to flag them [meyer2021]. Steger et al. showed that high AUC can coexist with geomorphically implausible maps, for example when a model learns artefacts of the inventory [steger2016]. Discrimination alone is therefore not enough evidence of a transferable, process-consistent model.

## 5. Transferability and domain shift in landslide models

Transferability has been tested mostly for rainfall-triggered landslides and across regions, not across earthquakes. Von Ruette et al. validated shallow-landslide models across different inventories and rainfall events and found performance degraded when models were moved to another event [vonruette2011]. Petschko et al. assessed the quality of susceptibility maps in Lower Austria, including how models behave across subregions [petschko2014]. Wang et al. framed regional transfer explicitly as transfer learning, using domain adaptation and case-based reasoning to adapt models to data-poor target areas [wang2022transfer]. The statistical basis is covariate shift: when the predictor distribution differs between training and target domains, importance weighting or re-estimation is needed [shimodaira2000]. In the coseismic setting the shift is large. Events differ in magnitude, rupture style, ground-motion uncertainty, climate, lithology and inventory protocol (Sections 1 and 3).

Transfer is also active in landslide *mapping* from imagery. Bhuyan et al. used a deep transfer-learning strategy to build multi-temporal inventories across sites [bhuyan2023]. However, transfer of a detection model across images is a different problem from transfer of a hazard model across earthquakes.

## 6. Physics-informed and explainable ML in geoscience

Reichstein et al. argued that data-driven Earth system science should be combined with process understanding through hybrid models and physical consistency, not pure pattern recognition [reichstein2019]. Karpatne et al. formalised "theory-guided data science": scientific knowledge enters through features, model structure, constraints and loss, which improves generalisation and physical consistency [karpatne2017]. For landslides, Dahal et al. moved toward physics-informed neural networks that embed slope-stability reasoning [dahal2025pinn]. Monotonic constraints in gradient boosting are a lightweight version of the same idea. Probability is forced to be non-decreasing in ground motion and slope, which rules out the spurious non-monotone responses that unconstrained learners can fit to inventory noise [chen2016; karpatne2017].

Lundberg et al. introduced TreeSHAP for exact Shapley-value attributions on tree ensembles and showed how local explanations combine into global understanding [lundberg2020]. Dahal & Lombardo reviewed explainable AI for landslide susceptibility and argued that attributions can reveal whether models learn physically meaningful controls [dahal2023xai]. Rudin cautioned that post-hoc explanations of black boxes can mislead in high-stakes settings and favoured inherently interpretable models [rudin2019]. Disaster response is such a setting. This tension motivates our study: whether SHAP attributions are stable across events is itself evidence of whether a model has learned transferable process (shaking × slope) rather than event-specific artefacts.

## 7. Probability calibration and model updating

NRT products are used as probabilities, for example to estimate landslide area or exposed population [nowickijessee2018; allstadt2022]. Calibration therefore matters as much as ranking. Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana showed that boosted trees produce systematically distorted probabilities that need post-hoc calibration (Platt scaling or isotonic regression) [niculescumizil2005]. The Brier score measures probabilistic accuracy [brier1950]. Van Calster et al. call calibration "the Achilles heel of predictive analytics" because models that discriminate well can still give badly miscalibrated risks in a new population [vancalster2019]. Clinical prediction research offers a mature toolkit for transferring models: intercept-only recalibration, logistic recalibration (intercept plus slope) and model revision. Their sample-size requirements and shrinkage behaviour are well studied [steyerberg2004; janssen2008]. These methods carry over directly to few-shot recalibration of a transferred landslide model from a handful of early-mapped landslides. In the landslide literature, such updating has been discussed qualitatively for emergency response [robinson2017] and for incomplete inventories [xu2013incomplete], but not, to our knowledge, benchmarked systematically across events.

## 8. Remote sensing for rapid landslide mapping and inputs

The few-shot scenario is realistic only if some landslides can be mapped quickly. Williams et al. reviewed satellite-based emergency mapping after the 2015 Nepal earthquakes. They documented that early optical mapping is fast but incomplete and limited by cloud [williams2018]. Automated detection is improving: deep learning versus traditional ML [prakash2020], U-Net detection in the Himalaya [meena2022], and multi-temporal transfer [bhuyan2023]. Cloud-independent SAR amplitude-change "heatmaps" in Google Earth Engine can flag landslide-dense areas within days [handwerger2022]. Together these support the assumption that tens of landslides can be confirmed within the first days. Terrain predictors come from the Copernicus GLO-30 DEM, which an independent evaluation against lidar and ICESat-2 found the most accurate of the global 1-arcsecond DEMs [guth2021].

## 9. Aotearoa New Zealand and Pacific Rim relevance

New Zealand has a long record of earthquake-induced landsliding linked to MM intensity [hancox2002]. Kaikōura showed how a multi-fault rupture [hamling2017] can produce tens of thousands of landslides [massey2020update] and test NRT models [allstadt2018; robinson2018kaikoura]. For a future Alpine Fault (AF8) earthquake, Robinson et al. estimated tens of thousands of coseismic landslides and large sediment volumes in the Southern Alps [robinson2016]. Project AF8 developed coordinated multi-agency response planning [orchiston2018]. Such planning needs landslide forecasts for an event with no inventory, which is exactly the transfer setting. Pacific Rim analogues (Palu, PNG, Chile, Japan, Alaska, California, Costa Rica) cover the tectonic, climatic and lithological variety such a forecast must survive.

## 10. Research gaps addressed by this study

Prior work has already done cross-event transfer, and we do not claim to be first. Global models were trained and tested on held-out events [nowicki2014; nowickijessee2018; tanyas2019slope]. A fuzzy-logic model was applied to unseen events [kritikos2015]. A Wenchuan model was applied to Lushan [li2013lushan]. Models were chrono-validated on later events [lombardo2020chrono] and tested out-of-sample on Kaikōura [allstadt2018]. Domain-adaptation transfer has been demonstrated for (mainly non-seismic) susceptibility [wang2022transfer]. The gaps below are narrower.

1. **No controlled, like-for-like leave-one-event-out comparison of global-style logistic, Newmark-physics, unconstrained ML and physics-constrained ML under identical inputs.** Existing cross-event tests evaluate one model family at a time, often with different predictors and validation units [nowickijessee2018; kritikos2015; tanyas2019slope; li2013lushan]. ML comparisons such as [merghadi2020] use random or within-region validation. It is therefore unknown whether the higher in-sample discrimination of gradient boosting survives transfer to a new earthquake, and whether monotonic physical constraints [karpatne2017; reichstein2019] close any gap. We address this with LOEO across nine Pacific Rim events using common GLO-30 and ShakeMap inputs.

2. **Discrimination and calibration are not separated under transfer.** Coseismic studies report mainly AUC [budimir2015; reichenbach2018]. Calibration failure is the expected way a transferred model breaks [vancalster2019; niculescumizil2005], and it determines the usefulness of probability-based products such as the USGS ground-failure product [allstadt2022]. We report AUC alongside Brier score, calibration intercept/slope and reliability diagrams for each held-out event.

3. **The value of a few rapidly mapped landslides for recalibrating a transferred model has not been quantified.** Emergency mapping supplies partial inventories within days [williams2018; handwerger2022], and incomplete inventories carry useful signal [xu2013incomplete]. Model-updating theory gives the tools [steyerberg2004; janssen2008]. Yet no study we found measures how many early landslides are needed to restore calibration of a transferred coseismic model, or whether physics-constrained models need fewer. We benchmark intercept-only and logistic recalibration as a function of the number of mapped landslides.

4. **Explanation stability across events is unexamined.** SHAP has been used to interpret landslide models within a single study area [dahal2023xai; lundberg2020], but whether attributions for shaking, slope and Newmark displacement stay consistent from event to event has not been tested. Consistent attributions would indicate a model has learned process rather than event-specific artefacts [steger2016; rudin2019]. We quantify cross-event attribution stability and relate it to transfer skill.

5. **Pacific Rim and NZ-relevant transfer evidence is thin.** Kaikōura-based evaluations [allstadt2018; robinson2018kaikoura] and AF8 scenario estimates [robinson2016] do not test how models trained elsewhere in the Pacific Rim transfer to NZ, or how unusual mechanisms such as the irrigation-enabled Palu flowslides [bradley2019palu] break models. Our LOEO design makes Kaikōura and Palu explicit out-of-distribution tests, with feature-space applicability checks [meyer2021].

**Caveats to state in the manuscript.** Differences in inventory completeness and mapping protocol between events [tanyas2017; schmitt2017] limit what LOEO scores can show, because part of the apparent transfer error is inventory error [steger2016]. ShakeMap uncertainty differs between events [worden2018], and richer shaking representations can improve prediction [dahal2024waveform]. Our scalar PGA/PGV inputs are a deliberate operational choice, not a claim of optimality.
